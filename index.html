01 · About

What is deforestation, and what causes it?

Deforestation is the clearing of forested land, cutting down trees so the land can be used for something else. It drives land-use change and can raise the risk of zoonotic disease spillover. Its main drivers are farming, mining, drilling and logging, including for paper and timber.

Where does forest loss come from?

Global Forest Watch splits 2001–2025 tree cover loss into permanent deforestation and temporary loss where trees can regrow. Tap a slice or a driver.

Deforestation (permanent) · 34% · ~182 M ha Temporary loss · 66% · ~359 M ha

Permanent agriculture accounts for about 172 M ha of the deforestation share, an area larger than Mongolia.

Main causes (from our research)

🌾 Agriculture expansion

Clearing land for crops and cattle pasture is the single biggest permanent driver. In Latin America it accounts for 72% of loss and in Southeast Asia 65%.

🪵 Commercial logging

Logging dominates tree cover loss in Europe (90%) and is a major share in North America (43%). In many managed forests, trees are replanted or regrow.

🏗 Infrastructure & mining

Roads, settlements and mines open up forests. In Madre de Dios, Peru, gold mining causes about 30% of loss. WRI Africa notes mining is a larger indirect driver in the Congo Basin than once recognised.

🔥 Wildfire

Fire caused 42% of global tree cover loss in 2025 and nearly half of tropical primary forest loss in 2024, a record. Fires in Russia and Canada are mostly in boreal forest, where regrowth is possible.

🌱 Shifting cultivation

Dominant in Africa (50%) and the DR Congo (83%): small plots cleared, farmed, then left to regrow.

Why this matters for climate: forests remove roughly 16 billion tonnes of CO₂ a year. Tree cover loss from logging and fire has averaged about 8.1 billion tonnes a year over two decades, so the net forest sink is about 7.6 billion tonnes a year. Southeast Asian rainforests have become a net CO₂ source because of high deforestation, while the Amazon and Congo basins remain net sinks.
02 · Effects

What are the effects?

Deforestation harms the environment and people at the same time. Select a card to see the mechanism and the evidence.

🌡 Climate change

Trees absorb CO₂. When they are cut or burned, the carbon is released. Fires alone emitted about 4.1 Gt of greenhouse gases in 2024, more than four times the emissions of all air travel in 2023.

🦜 Biodiversity loss

Forests are habitat for over 80% of terrestrial species. Losing them removes food, shelter and breeding grounds, which can lead to extinction.

🏜 Soil erosion

Roots hold soil in place. Without them, rain and wind strip rich topsoil, leaving land barren and silting up rivers.

💧 Water cycle

Trees release moisture through transpiration. Forested watersheds and wetlands supply about three-quarters of accessible freshwater.

🏚 Homelessness

Forces millions of Indigenous and local people off their lands when forests are cleared or sold.

🦠 Disease

Habitat disruption brings people closer to wildlife, raising the chance that animal viruses spill over to humans (zoonotic disease).

💸 Poverty

Many people depend on forests for food, fuel and income. The World Bank estimates 300–350 million people live in or near forests and largely depend on them, and over a billion rely on forests for jobs, products and income.

Research note. Our slides say “1.6 billion people” are stripped of food and income. That figure is widely quoted for forest dependence overall, but it describes people who rely on forests, not people who lose everything when forests shrink. We recommend wording it the way the World Bank does. See the fact-check table.

04 · Countries

What countries does it affect?

Every country loses some tree cover, but a handful account for most of it. Note that a country’s rank in tree cover loss is not the same as its rank in permanent deforestation. Russia and Canada top the list mostly because of boreal wildfire and logging, where forest can regrow.

Global Forest Watch also reports Brazil’s non-fire primary forest loss fell 41% in 2025, its lowest on record, but warns that growing fire risk could undo gains. Agência Brasil headlines the DETER fall as 36.87%; the two area figures it gives imply about 36%, so we say “roughly 36–37%”.

🇻🇳 Spotlight: Vietnam

Vietnam had about 14.87 million ha of forest on 31 December 2024: just over 10 M ha natural and nearly 5 M ha planted. The national forest-cover rate has been reported at 42.02% (2022 figure).

Natural forest ≈ 10 M haPlanted ≈ 5 M ha

Satellite-based tree cover loss in Vietnam in 2024 was about 128,000 ha, and tree cover loss includes harvest of plantations and other temporary loss, so it must not be confused with permanent deforestation. About a third of Vietnam’s forest area is planted forest.

Note. The first number counts all tree cover loss (including plantation harvest). The second counts forest area under national definitions. They answer different questions.
05 · Trends

Is it getting better or worse?

Both. Long-run deforestation is slowing, but recent years were punctuated by record fire-driven loss. The chart from our slide, “Rising Global Forest Loss (2001–2025)”, is reproduced below with its real values. The latest data add an important twist.

Timeline

  • 2001Annual satellite record of global tree cover loss begins.
  • 2013Hansen et al. publish the first high-resolution global forest change maps in Science.
  • 2016Brazil’s tree cover loss peaks in the series at 5.37 M ha.
  • 2021Glasgow Leaders’ Declaration: 140+ countries pledge to halt and reverse forest loss by 2030.
  • 2023Canada records its highest annual loss in the series: 8.57 M ha, driven by wildfire.
  • 2024Record year: 30 M ha of tree cover lost; fires drive nearly half of tropical primary forest loss.
  • Oct 2025FAO’s Global Forest Resources Assessment: deforestation slowed to 10.9 M ha/yr (2015–2025) from 17.6 M ha/yr in the 1990s.
  • Apr 2026GFW: tropical primary forest loss falls 36% in 2025 to 4.3 M ha, but fires remain a threat.
  • Aug 2026INPE: Amazon deforestation alerts hit their lowest level in the DETER series.
Off track for 2030. Current global forest loss is about 70% above the level needed to meet the 2030 goal. In 2024, WRI estimated deforestation would need to fall about 20% each year to get there.
06 · Perspectives

Two perspectives on deforestation

People disagree about how to weigh forests against economic needs. Understanding both helps explain why solutions are hard.

Protect the forest

This side opposes deforestation, arguing for the rights of Indigenous people who depend on forests for shelter and for the habitat of over 80% of terrestrial species. They point to carbon storage, clean water and disease risk as costs that fall on everyone.

Evidence they cite: forests hold 714 Gt of carbon; protected areas cover about 813 M ha (≈20% of forests).

Use the land to grow

This side supports clearing land for commercial crops, mining and energy, arguing that agriculture, minerals and infrastructure create jobs, food and income, particularly in lower-income countries that want to develop.

Evidence they cite: permanent agriculture is the main permanent driver of forest loss (about 172 M ha since 2001), and forests also employ people in forestry and timber.

Where the two sides can meet

  • Grow more on less land. Raising yields and restoring degraded pasture reduces pressure to clear new forest.
  • Pay for standing forests. Land rights, carbon finance and deforestation-free supply chains reward keeping trees.
  • Plant and manage sustainably. Planted forests are about 8% of the world’s forest area (≈312 M ha) and supply timber with less pressure on natural forest.

This summary is the team’s analysis, not a quotation from a source.

Methodology & limits

Tree cover loss ≠ deforestation. Global Forest Watch (University of Maryland) measures the removal of woody vegetation at least 5 m tall at 30 m resolution. It includes plantations, fire and logging where trees may regrow. FAO’s deforestation figures count permanent conversion to other land uses and are reported by countries. They should not be compared directly.
Official vs satellite numbers differ. Brazil’s PRODES (annual, official), DETER (near-real-time alerts) and UMD data use different definitions, minimum areas and reporting periods, so the percentage changes differ.
Rounding & comparisons. “Size of the UK/Denmark” are rough comparisons from the sources. The ~30 M ha global total for 2024 is a rounded figure.
Global series. The yearly global chart uses the team’s CSV for slide 8 (rounded to 0.1 M ha) and was cross-checked: it sums to the 2001–2025 total WRI reports. Brazil and Canada series come from Our World in Data (2001–2024).
